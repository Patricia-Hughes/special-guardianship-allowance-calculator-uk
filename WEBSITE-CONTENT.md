## The rules that are not style preferences

These come from the legal reviews and they hold whatever the new wording is.

1. **No em dashes.** A build gate fails on one. Commas, full stops, colons and
   brackets cover every case, and the rewrite that removes the dash is almost
   always the clearer sentence.
2. **Never say a council has broken the law.** Say what the guidance and the
   published decisions say, and what the reader can ask for. "Found at fault by
   the Ombudsman" is a fact. "Unlawful" is a finding a court makes.
3. **Never promise an amount.** Financial support is discretionary. There is no
   figure anyone is entitled to. The site shows what a council should be
   starting from.
4. **Regulation 7 is a power, not a protection.** It lets a council keep paying
   the reward element and lets it stop. Never "protected", never "guaranteed".
5. **Do not add a legal claim that is not already here.** Every claim on this
   site is tied to a source, and a build gate checks it. If the rewrite needs a
   new claim, flag it and it will be sourced first.
6. **Keep the caveats.** Where a sentence admits a gap or an assumption, that is
   deliberate. An admitted gap is more useful than an invisible one.

# What should your special guardianship allowance be?

Most special guardians are paid less than they should be, because councils apply blanket policies that have repeatedly been found wrong. This tool shows you the figure your council should be starting from, the rule it comes from, and what you can do about a gap.

- Free, and there is nothing to sign up for.
- Everything is worked out inside your own browser. Nothing you type is sent anywhere.
- Every figure has its source next to it, so you can check it or show someone.

**This is an estimate, not legal advice.** Financial support for special guardians is discretionary, there is no fixed amount you are entitled to. What this tool shows you is the figure your council should be starting from, and what the rules and published decisions say about how it must decide.

**The calculator needs JavaScript**, which is switched off in your browser. The full rate tables are below, and every guide on this site works without it.

## How councils are supposed to decide

- **Start with the fostering allowance.** Not an adoption allowance, and not a percentage of anything. [More](/guidance/what-your-council-must-consider)

# Who can get a special guardianship allowance?

Last updated 3 September 2026 · last reviewed 3 September 2026

Special guardianship orders are made under section 14A of the Children Act 1989. They exist in
England, Wales and Northern Ireland. They do not exist in Scots law, if you are in Scotland,
[kinship care works differently](/guidance/scotland-kinship-care).

[Children Act 1989, s.14A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1989/41/section/14A) (checked 2026-08-29)

## The question that decides how strong your position is

**Was the child in the council's care immediately before the order was made?**

**If yes**, and you ask, your council **must** assess your needs for support. This is not a
courtesy and it is not discretionary. The Children Act says a council may carry out an assessment
on request, and that it must do so where the person asking is of a description set out in
regulations.

[Children Act 1989, s.14F(3)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1989/41/section/14F) (checked 2026-09-03)
The regulations then name the people it must assess, and that list includes a special guardian of a
child who was looked after immediately before the order was made.

[Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 11](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1109/regulation/11) (checked 2026-09-03)
So the two go together: the Act creates the duty, and the regulation says who it applies to.

**If no**, and the child was not looked after, your council does not have to assess you. But it
cannot simply ignore you either. If it is minded not to carry out an assessment, it has to tell you
so, give its reasons, and give you a chance to make representations before it decides. That is
worth knowing, because "no" without reasons is not a proper answer even here.

Whether support is then payable is a separate question, and that is what regulation 6 governs.

[Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1109/regulation/6) (checked 2026-08-29)
If you are not sure which applies to you, ask your social worker or check the court paperwork. It
matters enough to be worth confirming.

## The money is up to the council, the decision is not

Even where an assessment is mandatory, there is no fixed amount you are entitled to. Financial
support under regulation 6 is discretionary, which means it is up to the council.

What you can expect is a proper decision:

- starting from the right figure, [the core fostering allowance](/guidance/what-your-council-must-consider)

- taking account of your circumstances and the child's needs

- explained to you in writing

- [reviewed every year](/guidance/annual-reviews)

## Which council

For three years from the making of the order, the council that looked after the child stays
responsible for assessing support needs, even if you have moved away.

[Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1109/regulation/5) (checked 2026-08-29)
More on that in [which council is responsible](/guidance/which-council-is-responsible).

## Common questions

The child was never in care. Can I still get support?

Yes. Where the child was not looked after immediately before the order, the council does not have to assess you, but it can, and you can ask it to. If it is minded to refuse, it has to give you its reasons and let you respond before it decides. Put the request in writing.

How do I know if the child was 'looked after'?

It means the child was in the council's care or accommodated by it immediately before the order was made. Your social worker or the court paperwork will confirm it.

# What your council must consider when setting your allowance

Last updated 29 August 2026 · last reviewed 29 August 2026 · rates for 2026/27

The single most useful thing to understand is this: **there is no amount you are entitled to, but
there is a figure your council is supposed to start from.**

## The starting point is the fostering allowance

The statutory guidance says the amount of a special guardianship allowance should not exceed the
amount the council would pay a foster carer for that child, and that the core fostering allowance
is the basis for the calculation.

[DfE Special Guardianship Guidance (Jan 2017), para 65](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/special-guardianship-guidance) (checked 2026-08-29)
That is why the fostering national minimum allowance matters to you, even though you are not a
foster carer:

**England: weekly minimum allowance, financial year 2026/27**

| Age of child | London | South East | Rest of England |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–2 | £205 | £195 | £176 |
| 3–4 | £208 | £203 | £182 |
| 5–10 | £233 | £223 | £201 |
| 11–15 | £266 | £255 | £227 |
| 16–17 | £309 | £298 | £267 |

## What has been ruled out

Three patterns have been challenged successfully in the courts:

**Using the adoption allowance model instead.** Adoption allowances are generally lower. A council
that used them as the basis for special guardianship support was found to have acted unlawfully.

[R (B) v Lewisham LBC [2008] EWHC 738 (Admin)](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWHC/Admin/2008/738.html) (checked 2026-09-03)
**Paying a flat two-thirds.** A council that set every special guardianship allowance at two-thirds
of the core fostering allowance was found to have departed from the guidance without justifying it.

[Barrett v Kirklees MBC [2010] EWHC 467 (Admin)](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWHC/Admin/2010/467.html) (checked 2026-09-03)
**Any blanket percentage.** A blanket percentage deduction was held unlawful. Importantly, the same
case confirmed there is no duty to pay every special guardian an allowance, and that a means-test
methodology can itself be perfectly lawful.

[R (TT) v Merton LBC [2012] EWHC 2055 (Admin)](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/admin/2012/2055) (checked 2026-09-03)
Important

The problem in each case was the *blanket* nature of the rule, not the arithmetic. A council can
reach a lower figure. It has to get there by considering your case.

## What the council should consider

Regulation 6 sets out what financial support is for and when it may be paid. In deciding the
amount, a council should be looking at:

- the child's needs, including any disability or the consequences of abuse or neglect

- your financial resources, including any income the child has

- what it costs you to look after the child

- the nature of the child's relationship with you, and the arrangement's stability

[Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 6](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1109/regulation/6) (checked 2026-08-29)

## What to ask for

Ask for the calculation in writing: the starting figure and where it came from, what income was
counted, what outgoings were allowed, and every deduction with its reason. A figure without a
breakdown cannot be checked, and a figure that cannot be checked cannot be challenged.

## Common questions

Am I entitled to an allowance?

No, financial support for special guardians is discretionary. What you are entitled to is a proper decision: one that starts from the right figure, considers your actual circumstances, and is explained to you.

Can my council pay me less than the fostering rate?

Yes, after properly assessing your circumstances. What it should not do is start from a lower figure, or apply a fixed reduction to everybody.

# How the means test works, and why it is not the law

Last updated 29 August 2026 · last reviewed 29 August 2026 · rates for 2026/27

Most English councils work out how much of your income to take into account using a standard
spreadsheet model produced by the Department for Education.

Important

That model is **not statutory**. No law requires it, and it was written before Universal Credit
existed. Treat any figure it produces as an illustration of how the common model works, not as the
answer your council will reach.

[CoramBAAF Practice Note 75 - special guardianship financial support](https://corambaaf.org.uk/sites/default/files/Members%20Area/Resources/Practice%20Notes/PN%2075%20Financial%20Support%20for%20Special%20Guardians_Englandv2.pdf) (checked 2026-09-03)
Here is how far behind it has fallen. The model measures your basic living costs against **Income
Support** rates. Income Support is a legacy benefit that is largely closed to new claims, and most
of the people it once covered are now on Universal Credit. So the yardstick at the centre of the
calculation is one that most households can no longer even claim.

At least one council publishes the model in its own words as non-statutory guidance, which is worth
knowing if yours presents it as a rule it has to follow.

[Lincolnshire County Council, Special Guardianship Allowance Assessment Framework](https://www.lincolnshire.gov.uk/directory-record/82363/special-guardianship-allowance-assessment-framework) (checked 2026-08-29)
It is also worth knowing that a means test is not in itself unlawful. When a blanket percentage
deduction was struck down, the court upheld the means-test methodology itself.

[R (TT) v Merton LBC [2012] EWHC 2055 (Admin)](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/admin/2012/2055) (checked 2026-09-03)

## The steps

- **Add up your countable income.** Earnings after tax, pensions, maintenance you receive, income
from a lodger, and most benefits.

- **Ignore 20% of it.** A fifth of your income is disregarded.

- **Subtract your allowable outgoings.** Rent or mortgage, council tax, childcare, essential loan
repayments, maintenance you pay out, pension contributions, plus a figure for basic living
costs, calculated as the Income Support rates for a household your size plus 25%.

- **What is left is your disposable income.**

- **Some or all of it is treated as your contribution** towards the child's costs, and taken off
the maximum.

## The mistake that costs people the most

Important, please read

The 20% disregard comes off your **income**, before your outgoings are subtracted. Not afterwards.

The difference is large. Take a household with £3,000 a month of income and £2,000 a month of
allowable outgoings:

- **Correct:** £3,000 × 0.8 = £2,400, then − £2,000 = **£400** disposable

- **Wrong:** £3,000 − £2,000 = £1,000, then × 0.8 = **£800** disposable

Done the wrong way round, this household is assessed as able to contribute twice as much, and the
allowance drops accordingly. If you have a written breakdown, check which order yours was done in.

## What counts and what does not

**Counted:** earnings (both partners), self-employed drawings, employer sick pay, pensions, most
benefits, Child Benefit for your *other* children, savings interest, lodger and property income,
maintenance you receive.

**Ignored:** disability benefits for the child (DLA or PIP), Guardian's Allowance, criminal
injuries compensation, fostering income for other children you still foster, the special
guardianship allowance itself, and the childcare element of Working Tax Credit.

The Child Benefit rule confuses almost everybody, including social workers: Child Benefit for your
**other** children counts as income; Child Benefit for the child this assessment is about does not.
That child's Child Benefit is only ever dealt with as a separate deduction, if at all, and whether
it should be deducted is [itself contested](/guidance/child-benefit-deduction).

## The step nobody publishes

There is **no published national rule** for how much of your disposable income a council takes off
the allowance. Some take it pound for pound, some take a percentage, some ignore anything under a
threshold. Policies genuinely differ, and no one has published a national figure.

So ask yours which it uses. If a figure has been put to you, that question alone often produces the
breakdown you have been asking for.

## Common questions

Does my council have to use this model?

No. It is not statutory. Some councils use it, some adapt it, and some do an individual needs assessment instead. What a council cannot do is apply any model so rigidly that it never looks at your actual circumstances.

Is my special guardianship allowance counted as my income for benefits?

No. It is disregarded for your own means-tested benefits, and it is not taxable.

Are my child's disability benefits counted?

They should not be. Disability Living Allowance or Personal Independence Payment for the child is disregarded income, as is Guardian's Allowance.

## Two different calendars

Important

There are three dates in April, not two. Councils normally raise allowance rates on **1 April**.
The government states those same rates for a **tax year running 6 April to 5 April**. Benefit rates
change in the **first full week of April**, around the 6th. Between the 1st and the 6th, the new

allowances and benefits as two independent lookups with their own effective dates, so a calculation

# Why a fixed percentage of the fostering rate is a problem

Last updated 29 August 2026 · last reviewed 29 August 2026

If someone has told you your allowance is "two-thirds of the fostering rate", or a third, or any
other flat fraction, this is worth reading.

## The short answer

Whether your council's particular policy is lawful depends on its wording and on how it is applied.
No court has said that fixed percentages are unlawful as a class. Each case that reached a court
concerned one council's policy on its own facts. So the thing to ask for is the written policy and
the reasons in your case, not a ruling.

## What the courts have said

A council that set every special guardianship allowance at a flat two-thirds of the core fostering
allowance was found to have departed from the statutory guidance without justification. In plainer
words, it stepped away from a rule it is supposed to follow, without explaining why. That is where
the unlawfulness lies, and it is why the phrase matters more than it sounds.

[Barrett v Kirklees MBC [2010] EWHC 467 (Admin)](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWHC/Admin/2010/467.html) (checked 2026-09-03)
A blanket percentage deduction was held unlawful in that council's case in a later judgment. The
same judgment confirmed two things that cut the other way, and they matter just as much: there is no
duty to pay every special guardian an allowance at all, and a means test is not in itself unlawful.

# Can your council take Child Benefit off your special guardianship allowance?

Last updated 3 September 2026 · last reviewed 3 September 2026 · rates for 2026/27

If your allowance seems low, this is one of the first things worth checking. It is also one of the
most commonly contested.

## Start with what is allowed

The regulations **require** a council to take into account any other grant, benefit, allowance or
resource available to you. They do not name Child Benefit, but Child Benefit is plainly one of
them, so it is something your council has to consider.

[Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 13(2)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1109/regulation/13) (checked 2026-09-03)
The deduction itself comes from the case law rather than from that wording. In *Barrett v Kirklees*
the court said councils should pay at a rate in line with the fostering allowance, "with the
deduction of Child Benefit if appropriate".

[Barrett v Kirklees MBC [2010] EWHC 467 (Admin)](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWHC/Admin/2010/467.html) (checked 2026-09-03)
So the deduction is permitted. If you ask your council whether it is allowed to do this, the answer
is yes, and the conversation stops there. That is why this page starts here rather than with the
complaint.

It is worth being precise about this, because a council that is quoted the wrong rule will correct
you, and you will have spent your first letter losing a point you did not need to make.

## What some councils do

A number of councils treat Child Benefit as income when they work out a special guardianship
allowance, and take it off pound for pound. So a carer who would otherwise get the full amount
receives that much less each week.

In 2024 the Local Government and Social Care Ombudsman looked at a council that deducted Child

individual circumstances. The Ombudsman found that at fault. The council had to recalculate the
allowances of around 170 carers, pay refunds, and make remedy payments to the carers who

# If you fostered the child before the order: the two-year rule

Last updated 29 August 2026 · last reviewed 29 August 2026 · rates for 2026/27

If you were the child's foster carer before the special guardianship order was made, there is a rule
that applies to you and not to other special guardians. The end of it is not automatic and it is not
final, which is the part most people are not told.

## What regulation 7 does

For **two years** from the date of the order, your council can carry on paying the reward or fee
element of the fostering allowance you were receiving for that child, and for longer where it
considers that appropriate.

Read that carefully: it says **can**, not **must**. Regulation 7 gives your council a power rather
than giving you an entitlement. Your position is still a strong one, but the argument comes from the
statutory guidance and from the Court of Appeal holding that family foster carers should not be
worse off than unrelated ones, not from regulation 7 creating a right.

[Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1109/regulation/7) (checked 2026-08-29)
Our calculator applies this automatically. If you tell us what you were paid as a foster carer and
the order was made less than two years ago, we hold your figure at that amount rather than showing
you a lower one, even where the national minimum for your area is lower.

## Why it exists

The Court of Appeal held that paying family foster carers less than unrelated foster carers was
unlawful, and that a carer should not be made worse off by taking on a special guardianship order
for a child they were already looking after.

[R (X) v Tower Hamlets LBC [2013] EWCA Civ 904](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2013/904) (checked 2026-09-03)

## The mistake to watch for

Important

Regulation 7 is about the **reward element**. It is not permission to step the whole allowance down.

In 2020 the Ombudsman looked at a council that reduced carers' entire allowances to 75% and then
50% over the two years, treating regulation 7 as authority to do so. The Ombudsman found that
misapplied the rule.

[LGSCO decision, Southend-on-Sea (19 003 363, 5 October 2020)](https://www.lgo.org.uk/decisions/children-s-care-services/friends-and-family-carers/19-003-363) (checked 2026-08-31)
If your payments are being stepped down, ask your council in writing **which element it is
reducing** and on what basis. That single question often resolves it.

## First, a question that comes before all of this

Regulation 7 only applies at all where your council decided to include a reward element in your
financial support **before the special guardianship order was made**. If it never took that
decision, regulation 7 is not the rule for your situation, and an argument built on it will not
land.

So the first thing to ask is simply whether that decision was taken, and when. If it was not, your
position rests on the statutory guidance and on the Tower Hamlets case instead, which say a council
should not pay family carers less than unrelated foster carers without cogent reasons.

## Before the two years are up

The end of the two years is not automatic and it is not final. But the test for continuing is
higher than it sounds. A council may carry on paying the reward element only where it considers
that necessary because of the **exceptional needs of the child, or other exceptional
circumstances**.

That wording matters. "My child's needs have not reduced" does not engage the test, and a request
put that way can be refused without the council ever having to weigh anything. Set out what is
exceptional about your child's needs or your circumstances, and say so in those terms.

Ask in good time, several months before the anniversary.

Does the two-year rule cover my whole allowance?

No. Regulation 7 is about the reward or fee element, the part that paid you for your work as a foster carer. It is not authority to step your entire allowance down over two years. If your whole allowance is being reduced, ask which part is being reduced and on what basis.

What happens after two years?

It cuts both ways. The reward element can lawfully stop, and it can also lawfully carry on: the council can agree to continue it where it considers that appropriate. Nothing happens automatically, so ask before the two years are up rather than after your payments drop.

I fostered the child but through a different council. Does it still apply?

Two rules that catch people out, and one benefit that is widely missed.

## Your allowance is not taxed, and does not reduce your benefits

Special guardianship allowance is treated as a residence-order allowance. It has been **exempt from
income tax** since 6 April 2010, and it is **disregarded** as your income when your own means-tested
benefits are worked out.

If your Universal Credit dropped when your allowance started, that is worth querying.

Important

**Qualifying Care Relief does not apply to special guardians.** It is a relief for foster carers
and shared-lives carers. If you also still foster other children, you can use it for that income,
but not for your special guardianship allowance.

## Guardian's Allowance, check whether you can claim it

This is the most commonly missed payment in kinship care.

Guardian's Allowance is a weekly payment for someone bringing up a child whose parents have died,
or where one parent has died and the other cannot be traced, is serving a long prison sentence, or
whose whereabouts are unknown.

It is worth £22.95 a week (2026-27) for each child, on top of anything else
you receive.

- It is **tax free**.

- It is **ignored** for means-tested benefits.

- It is **exempt from the benefit cap**.

[Child Benefit and Guardian's Allowance Up-rating Order 2026](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/232/made) (checked 2026-09-03)
There are conditions, including about the parents' UK residence. It is worth a conversation with an
adviser if there is any chance it applies.

# Which council is responsible for your support?

Last updated 3 September 2026 · last reviewed 3 September 2026

This catches people out, particularly where a family has moved to be closer to relatives after an
order was made.

## The three-year rule

For **three years** from the making of the special guardianship order, the council that was looking
after the child immediately before the order remains responsible for assessing support needs, even
if you now live somewhere else entirely.

[Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2005/1109/regulation/5) (checked 2026-08-29)
After three years, responsibility normally passes to the council for the area where you live.

## The exception, and it is an important one

If your council was **already paying you financial support**, and it decided to pay it **before**
the order was made, then the three-year cut-off does not apply to that support. The original
council stays responsible for it, indefinitely.

That will often be the case if you were the child's foster carer before the order, because the
support was usually agreed as part of the arrangements at the time.

So if you have been told your support ends, or passes to a new council, because three years have
gone by, the question to ask is: **when was the decision to provide my financial support taken, and
was it before the order was made?**

Important

Financial support that has already been agreed is a separate question from who assesses new needs.
If you are being passed back and forth, ask each council to confirm its position in writing.

## Why it is worth pinning down early

Every request you make, for a review, for an enhancement, for the written policy, has to go to
the right council to get an answer. Time spent asking the wrong one is time your allowance stays
where it is.

# About this site

Most special guardians are paid less than they should be. Not usually because anyone intended it, but because councils apply blanket policies that have repeatedly been found wrong, and because almost nobody being paid too little knows what the right figure looks like.

This site exists to close that gap.

## Our editorial policy

### Every figure traces to a source

Rates live in versioned data files with an effective date, not in the text of a page. When the April uprating lands, every figure across the site changes at once. A number typed into prose goes silently wrong every April, so our build refuses to accept one.

### We do not publish what we have not verified

Where we cannot confirm a figure against its original source, we leave it out and say why. That is why we currently publish no Welsh rates, and no 2024/25 table. Working a historic rate out from an uplift percentage would give a number that looks precise and could be wrong by a few pounds, and a few pounds across two years is exactly what gets a carer's complaint dismissed.

### We do not name a council without a source

Saying what a particular council does, especially where an Ombudsman has criticised it, requires a stored, dated, public source. Where we hold information about a council but have not re-sourced the link, our calculator ignores it entirely and uses the national figures instead. We currently cover 153 English authorities for regional rates.

### Our checks run before anything is published

- Rates must rise with age, sit in a sane range, and move plausibly year on year.
- Legal test cases covering the rules that must never break are run on every change, and cannot be skipped.
- The calculator refuses to output a fixed percentage of the fostering rate, ever.
- If our rates go past their end date, a warning appears on every page automatically.

### Review schedule

Each of our 16 guides carries a “last reviewed” date and a review interval, and we are told when one falls overdue. Rate data is reviewed every April; the Kinship Zones pilot every three months; council information annually.

## What we are not

We are not a law firm, a charity, or part of any council or government department. We do not give advice on individual cases. Where your situation needs that, the advice organisations we point to throughout the site can help, and they are independent of us.

## Corrections

If we have something wrong, we want to know. See our [corrections policy](/contact).

# Disclaimer

## This is not legal advice

Nothing on this site is legal advice, and using it does not create any professional relationship. It gives general information about how special guardianship allowances are supposed to be decided. It cannot tell you what will happen in your case.

## Every figure is an estimate

Financial support for special guardians is **discretionary**. There is no fixed amount you are entitled to. What the calculator shows you is the figure the statutory guidance says your council should be starting from, not a figure you can require it to pay.

A council can lawfully pay less than the maximum after properly assessing your circumstances. What it should not do is start from the wrong figure, or apply a blanket rule without considering your case.

## What we do not know about your council

Unless the result said otherwise, the calculator uses national minimum figures for your area. It does not know your council's own policy, its enhancement rates, or how it applies its means test. Those vary a great deal.

## Where our data has gaps, we say so

We do not publish figures we have not verified against their original source, and we do not work missing figures out from percentages. A number that looks precise and is slightly wrong is worse than no number, because you might take it to your council.

So **working out what you were underpaid in past years is not here yet**. It needs the 2024/25 rate tables as they were published, and we have not been able to find them. We could get close by taking this year’s figures and removing the increase, but close is not good enough for a claim you are going to put to your council.

The **Welsh rates** are here, with one caveat we would rather you heard from us: in Wales the rate for a child under 5 is higher than for a child aged 5 to 15. That is genuinely how they are published, and we have checked it against two years of figures, but we have not seen the Welsh Government’s own circular, only reports of it.

## Rates change

Allowance rates change on 1 April each year and benefit rates change a few days later. Check the date on any page before you rely on it. If our rates are out of date, a banner at the top of every page will tell you.

## Please check anything that matters

If something here could affect a decision about money or about your child, check it with an adviser before you act on it.

# Privacy notice

**We do not collect, store or process any personal information about you or your child.** That is not a policy promise. It is how the site is built.

## How the calculator works

Everything the calculator does happens inside your own browser. There is no server that performs the calculation, and therefore nothing for your answers to be sent to. Your child's age, your income, your savings, your benefits, none of it leaves the device you are reading this on.

We built it this way deliberately. Financial and family circumstances about a child are about as sensitive as personal data gets, and the safest way to protect information is not to receive it.

## The one thing that is sent anywhere

If you use the “find my council” button, we send the first part of your postcode (like SW1A) to [postcodes.io](https://postcodes.io/), a free public service, to look up which council covers that area. That is the only outbound request carrying anything you typed.

- It only happens when you press the button. Never as you type.
- Only the outward code is sent, not the full postcode.
- Nothing else you have entered is sent with it.
- We use the answer and discard it. We do not store it, log it, or put it in the web address.
- You never have to use it. Choosing your council or region from the list works just as well.

## Cookies

We do not set any cookies. There is no cookie banner because there is nothing to consent to.

## Analytics

We do not run analytics on this site. Nothing counts your visit, nothing follows you between pages, and nothing is sent anywhere when you use the calculator.

That is a decision rather than an oversight, taken in August 2026 and checked automatically every time this site is built. If it ever changes, it will be a cookieless tool that never records anything you type, and this page will say so before it happens.

## Sharing a result

The share link contains your nation, region or council, your children's ages, and whether you were a foster carer. It never contains anything about your finances, your benefits, or your postcode. Web addresses end up in browser history, server logs and messaging app previews, so we keep the sensitive things out of them entirely.

## Saved progress

We do not save your progress. If you refresh the page you will start again. That is deliberate: shared and family computers are the normal case for the people who use this site.

## Your rights

Data protection law gives you rights over personal data an organisation holds about you. Since we hold none, there is nothing for us to give you access to, correct, or delete. If you think that is wrong, please [get in touch](/contact) and we will look into it.

# Accessibility statement

Most people who use this site are grandparents and other relatives in their fifties, sixties and seventies. Accessibility here is the core use case, not a compliance exercise.

## What we aim for

We aim to meet **WCAG 2.2 Level AA** on every page, and we exceed it in a few places where it matters for this audience.

- Body text is 18px, never below 16px anywhere, including footnotes and disclaimers.
- Body text contrast is at least 7:1, which is the AAA threshold rather than AA's 4.5:1.
- The site works at 200% zoom, and at 320px wide, with no sideways scrolling.
- Every button and link is at least 48×48 pixels, with space around it.
- Focus outlines are always visible and are never removed.
- Everything works with a keyboard alone. There are no keyboard traps and no time limits.
- Money fields are plain text fields, not number spinners, which are awkward with a screen reader and with shaky hands.
- We respect your system setting for reduced motion. Nothing moves or advances on its own.

## critical (5)


### Taking Child Benefit off your allowance may not be lawful in your case

_Code `UNLAWFUL_CB_DEDUCTION_RISK`, source: Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 13(2)_

You have told us you get a means-tested benefit **and** that your council takes Child Benefit off your allowance.

The regulations require your council to take account of other benefits available to you, and Child Benefit is one of them. The courts have contemplated it being deducted. What is not allowed is applying the same rule to everybody without looking at individual circumstances.

In 2024 the Local Government and Social Care Ombudsman found a council at fault for deducting Child Benefit from every special guardian on Universal Credit as a blanket policy. That council had to recalculate around 170 carers' allowances and pay refunds. It changed its policy, and a later complaint about the new practice was not upheld.

So the question is not whether your council can deduct Child Benefit. It is whether it decided your case individually, and what its reasons were. Ask for those reasons in writing.

The Ombudsman normally expects a complaint within 12 months of you becoming aware of a problem.


### Your council may be starting from the wrong figure

_Code `FIXED_PERCENTAGE_POLICY`, source: Barrett v Kirklees MBC [2010] EWHC 467 (Admin)_

[COUNCIL]'s published policy appears to set the allowance at a fixed share of the fostering rate, as we read it on [DATE].

The courts have held that applying a flat percentage, two-thirds or a third or any other fixed fraction, without looking at the individual case, is an unjustified departure from the guidance. In other words, the council has stepped away from a rule it is supposed to follow without explaining why. The starting point should be the core fostering allowance, and your own circumstances should then be considered.

This does not mean you are owed the full fostering rate. In the Merton case the court also held that a council does not have to pay every special guardian an allowance, and that means testing one is lawful. The problem is the blanket nature of a fixed fraction, not the arithmetic.

Ask for the written policy and for the reasons behind the figure you were given.


### This rate is below the national minimum for fostering

_Code `BELOW_NMA`, source: DfE Special Guardianship Guidance (Jan 2017), para 65_

The rate we hold for [COUNCIL] (£200 a week) is below the national minimum fostering allowance for the 11-15 age band in your area (£227 a week).

The statutory guidance says the core fostering allowance is the starting point for a special guardianship allowance. A figure below it needs an explanation.

Two things this does not mean. It is a minimum for fostering allowances, not a floor your own allowance cannot go below. And it is about the figure your council starts from, not the amount you end up with: a lower final figure after a means test can be perfectly proper, while a lower starting figure is the thing worth asking about.

Ask the council to explain in writing how it arrived at the figure and how it compares with what it pays its foster carers.

[WHAT THE FINDING SAID]

The decision was made on [DATE]. You can read it here: [LINK]

A published decision about one carer does not automatically apply to you, but it is relevant background if you are asking the council to look at your case again.

Treat the figures below as last year's. Check the current national minimum allowance before you use them in a letter.


## important (15)


### Your council can keep paying your old fostering rate for two years

_Code `REG7_FLOOR_APPLIED`, source: Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 7_

You fostered this child before the order, and the order was made 18 months ago.

For the first two years your council can carry on paying the reward part of what you were paid as a foster carer. Our estimate assumes it does, so we have shown £300 a week rather than a lower figure. That is what we have assumed, not a figure your council has agreed.

The Court of Appeal has held that family foster carers should not be worse off than unrelated foster carers, which is the argument for continuing it.


### The two-year point is coming up

_Code `REG7_EXPIRING`, source: Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 7_

The order was made 18 months ago. The two years during which your council can keep paying the reward part of your former fostering allowance end at 24 months.

It cuts both ways. After two years the reward element can lawfully stop, and it can also lawfully carry on: the council can agree to continue it where it considers that appropriate. Ask now, in writing, rather than after your payments drop.


### You can ask for the reward element to carry on past two years

_Code `REG7_EXTENSION_AVAILABLE`, source: Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 7_

Two years is not a hard stop. Your council can carry on paying the reward element beyond it, but only where it considers that necessary because of the **exceptional needs of the child, or other exceptional circumstances**.

That is a higher bar than it sounds, so it is worth putting your case in those terms rather than saying the needs have not reduced.

There is also a question that comes first: this rule only applies where your council decided to include a reward element **before** the order was made. Ask whether it did.


### Check what your council is tapering

_Code `REG7_MISAPPLICATION_WARNING`, source: LGSCO decision, Southend-on-Sea (19 003 363, 5 October 2020)_

Regulation 7 is about the **reward** part of a former foster carer's allowance, which a council can keep paying for two years. It does not authorise stepping down the whole allowance.

The Ombudsman found in 2020 that reducing an entire allowance to 75% and then 50% misapplied that rule. If your payments are being stepped down, ask the council which element it is reducing and why.


### Your assessment is not automatic

_Code `NOT_LOOKED_AFTER_BEFORE_ORDER`, source: Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 6_

Because the child was not in the council's care immediately before the order, the circumstances in which financial support is payable are narrower. It is discretionary, which means it is up to the council. That does not shut the door: it can still consider your case, and you can ask.

Put the request in writing and ask for the decision and its reasons in writing too.


### Your council is in the Kinship Zones pilot

_Code `KINSHIP_ZONE_ELIGIBLE`, source: Kinship Zones - kinship allowance pilot (2026-2029)_

[COUNCIL] is one of seven councils in the Kinship Zones pilot. In the pilot, eligible carers get an allowance equal to the full fostering National Minimum Allowance, and it is **not** means tested.

Ask your council whether you qualify, and ask for the answer in writing.


### The pilot is time-limited and has conditions

_Code `KINSHIP_ZONE_PILOT_CAVEAT`, source: Kinship Zones - kinship allowance pilot (2026-2029)_

Kinship Zones runs from April 2026 to 2029 in seven councils only. It does not change the law anywhere else.

You cannot get the pilot allowance and regulation 6 financial support for the same child at the same time. The scheme may change when it is evaluated.


### You can ask for more because of your child's needs

_Code `ENHANCEMENT_AVAILABLE`, source: Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 6_

You told us your child has a disability or additional needs. The regulations let a council pay more than the core allowance to meet the consequences of a disability, or of abuse or neglect the child suffered.

There is **no national figure** for this and we have not added one to your estimate. It is decided council by council, on the child's needs. Ask for it, and give specifics: therapy, travel, respite, equipment, extra wear and tear.


### The means test model is not law, and it pre-dates Universal Credit

_Code `MEANS_TEST_MODEL_OUTDATED`, source: CoramBAAF Practice Note 75 - special guardianship financial support_

Most English councils use a standard Department for Education spreadsheet model to work out how much of your income to take into account. That model is **not statutory**. No law requires it, and it was written before Universal Credit existed.

Your council may use a different method, and it is entitled to. Treat the figure below as an illustration of how the common model works, not as the answer your council will reach.


### We had to assume how your council reduces the allowance

_Code `TAPER_METHOD_ASSUMED`, source: CoramBAAF Practice Note 75 - special guardianship financial support_

There is **no published national rule** for how much of your spare income a council takes off the allowance. Policies vary.

We have assumed pound-for-pound, which is the most common approach. Your council may use another.

Ask it two things: which method it uses, **and where that comes from**. There being no national rule is not a gap in our research, it is how the scheme is built, so a council that cannot say where its own taper comes from is in a weaker position than one that can.


### Plan now for what happens at 18

_Code `AGE_17_TRANSITION`, source: Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 9_

Your child is 17. Special guardianship financial support normally stops when they turn 18, unless they stay in full-time education or training, in which case it can continue.

Ask your council now, in writing, what will happen on their eighteenth birthday and what you need to do to keep support going.


### The Scottish Recommended Allowance is a minimum, not a maximum

_Code `SCOTLAND_SRA_IS_A_FLOOR`, source: Scottish Recommended Allowance for foster and kinship carers, Scottish Government, page updated 11 May 2026_

The Scottish Recommended Allowance is the floor. Your council can pay more, and some do.

If you are being offered less than the recommended allowance, ask why in writing.


### Kinship carers and foster carers get the same recommended rate

_Code `SCOTLAND_FOSTER_KINSHIP_PARITY`, source: Scottish Recommended Allowance for foster and kinship carers, Scottish Government, page updated 11 May 2026_

Scotland sets one recommended allowance for foster carers and kinship carers alike. If your council pays its foster carers more than it pays you for a child of the same age, ask it to explain the difference.


### The Ombudsman made a finding about a policy this council has since changed

_Code `LGSCO_FINDING_FORMER_POLICY`_

[WHAT THE FINDING SAID]

The decision was made on [DATE]. You can read it here: [LINK]

This is history rather than a live problem, but it is worth knowing. Where a council has been found at fault, the Ombudsman has often asked it to go back and recalculate old awards, so it can still be relevant to an order made some years ago.


### Your council allows a different amount for basic living costs

_Code `LA_LIVING_COSTS_VARIATION`, source: CoramBAAF Practice Note 75 - special guardianship financial support_

The standardised model most councils use allows basic living costs at 125% of the Income Support rate for a household your size. [COUNCIL] publishes 100%, and that is the figure used above.

Allowing less for living costs leaves more income counted as spare, which means a larger contribution expected from you and a smaller allowance. It is a local choice, not a national rule.

If the figure matters to you, ask the council to confirm the percentage it applied and why.


## info (14)


### Worth checking: was the child in care just before the order?

_Code `LOOKED_AFTER_STATUS_UNKNOWN`, source: Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 6_

You told us you were not sure. It matters, because it changes how strong your position is. If the child was in the council's care immediately before the order, you are squarely within the circumstances where financial support is payable. If not, those circumstances are narrower, though you can still ask.

Your social worker or the court paperwork will say.


### You are in a pilot area but already on regulation 6 support

_Code `KINSHIP_ZONE_EXCLUDED_REG6`, source: Kinship Zones - kinship allowance pilot (2026-2029)_

Your council is in the Kinship Zones pilot, but the pilot excludes carers already receiving regulation 6 financial support, so we have used the normal rules for your estimate.

That comparison is worth raising with your council if your current support is lower.


### What the pilot rate would be

_Code `KINSHIP_ZONE_COMPARISON`, source: Kinship Zones - kinship allowance pilot (2026-2029)_

The Kinship Zones pilot rate for your child's age band is the amount a week, not means tested.


### You are in the few days of April where the rules disagree

_Code `APRIL_RATE_BOUNDARY`, source: North Tyneside Council, Decision Record: Foster Care, Special Guardianship, Adoption and Child Arrangement Order Allowance Increases_

The government sets these rates for a **tax year running 6 April to 5 April**. Most councils put their increase in from **1 April**.

Between 1 and 5 April the two do not line up. We have shown you the new rate, because that is what councils normally pay from 1 April. But if your council follows the tax year, it may still be paying last year's rate for a few more days.

It is worth a few pounds, not a few hundred. If you are checking a backdated claim, it is worth getting right.


### In Wales, under-5s get more than 5 to 15 year olds

_Code `WALES_BAND_ORDER_ANOMALY`, source: Welsh Government, National Minimum Allowances for foster carers 2024 to 2025, circular of 26 February 2024_

The Welsh rates pay **more for a child under 5 than for a child aged 5 to 15**. Everywhere else in the UK the rate rises with age, so this looks like a mistake.

It is not a mistake on this site. That is how the Welsh Government publishes them, and the same pattern appears in other years too.

We have now checked it against the Welsh Government's own circular, which prints the rates that way. We are telling you rather than quietly correcting it.


### Your council may have a savings rule

_Code `CAPITAL_RULE_MAY_APPLY`, source: DWP, Benefit and pension rates 2026 to 2027_

You told us about savings of £7,000. Some councils count savings above a threshold. A common pattern is £6,000, then £1 a week for each £250 above it. But there is no national rule and we do not hold your council's.

We have not applied any savings rule to your estimate. Ask your council whether it has one.


### Your allowance should be reviewed every year

_Code `ANNUAL_REVIEW_DUE`, source: Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 18(2)(a)_

Your council has to review your financial support when it receives your annual statement of circumstances. The review is triggered by that statement, not simply by a year going by.

So if your council has never asked you for an annual statement, that is the thing to raise. Ask whether it wants one, and if not, why your support has not been reviewed. Rates go up every April.


### Which council is responsible

_Code `THREE_YEAR_RESPONSIBILITY`, source: Special Guardianship Regulations 2005, reg 5_

For three years after the order, the council that looked after the child stays responsible for assessing support needs, even if you have moved away. After three years, responsibility usually passes to the council where you live.

**There is an important exception.** If your council was already paying you financial support, and it decided to pay it **before** the order was made, then it stays responsible for that support after the three years are up. It does not pass to your new council. That will often be the case if you were the child's foster carer.

If you have moved recently, it is worth being clear which council you should be asking.


### Check whether the benefit cap affects you

_Code `BENEFIT_CAP_RISK`, source: DWP, Benefit and pension rates 2026 to 2027_

Households getting a lot of benefit income can hit the benefit cap. Special guardianship allowance itself is not counted as your income for your own means-tested benefits, and Guardian's Allowance is exempt from the cap.

If your benefits have been reduced without explanation, this is worth checking with an adviser.


### The two-child limit no longer applies

_Code `TWO_CHILD_LIMIT_ABOLISHED`, source: Universal Credit (Removal of Two Child Limit) Act 2026 c.13_

The two-child limit in Universal Credit was removed from April 2026. If you are caring for three or more children and your Universal Credit has not changed, check your award.


### Check whether you can claim Guardian's Allowance

_Code `GUARDIANS_ALLOWANCE_CHECK`, source: Child Benefit and Guardian's Allowance Up-rating Order 2026_

Guardian's Allowance is a weekly payment for someone bringing up a child whose parents have died, or where one has died and the other cannot be traced, is serving a long prison sentence, or cannot be found.

It is tax free, it is ignored for means-tested benefits, and it is exempt from the benefit cap. It is widely missed.


### We do not hold your council's published policy

_Code `COUNCIL_POLICY_UNKNOWN`, source: DfE, Kinship Care: statutory guidance for local authorities (October 2024)_

We do not yet hold a sourced, dated copy of [COUNCIL]'s special guardianship financial support policy, so your estimate uses the national minimum figures for your area.

Every council in England has to publish a kinship local offer setting out the support it provides, and most publish a financial support policy as well. Asking for it in writing is a good first step, and it is the document any later challenge rests on.


### This council is rewriting its policy

_Code `LA_POLICY_UNDER_REVIEW`_

[COUNCIL] says its special guardianship financial support policy is under review, so what is shown here is the last version it published.

Ask the council for its current position in writing before you rely on any figure here.


### No family should be worse off

_Code `SCOTLAND_NO_WORSE_OFF`, source: Scottish Recommended Allowance for foster and kinship carers, Scottish Government, page updated 11 May 2026_

Where you already have an arrangement that pays more than the recommended allowance, the higher amount applies. Moving to the recommended rate should not reduce what you get.

# The nine letters

Sent by a carer to their council, pre-filled with their own figures. They go to a
social worker the carer will deal with for years, so they are polite, short and
specific, and they always request rather than demand.

**Reviewed by a solicitor on 1 and 2 September 2026.** Several sentences are
precisely worded to avoid handing a council an easy correction. Flag anything you
want to change rather than changing it.
